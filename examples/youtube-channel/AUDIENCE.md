# AUDIENCE.md — YouTube channel about calm home cooking

## Audience name

Busy home cooks who want reliable weeknight meals, better kitchen judgment, and less recipe anxiety without chef theatrics or perfectionist food content.

## Summary

This channel serves people who cook because they need to eat well, feed others, save money, or reduce delivery dependence. They enjoy good food but do not want a personality-driven chaos show, luxury ingredient flex, or shame about shortcuts. They value practical technique, substitutions, cleanup reality, and repeatable meals.

## Primary audiences

### 1. Weeknight cooks with limited time and attention

People cooking after work, study, childcare, or errands who need dinner to be achievable with normal ingredients and imperfect energy.

**Needs**

- recipes with realistic active time and cleanup
- substitutions for common missing ingredients
- clear visual cues for doneness, texture, and seasoning
- shopping and prep guidance that reduces decision fatigue

**Constraints**

- limited time, budget, counter space, and energy
- picky eaters, dietary restrictions, or household preferences
- inconsistent equipment and pantry staples
- frustration with recipes that hide complexity

**Current alternatives or behaviors**

- searching YouTube or TikTok at the last minute
- rotating the same few safe meals
- ordering delivery when cooking feels too complicated
- saving recipes they rarely make

### 2. Beginner-to-intermediate cooks building kitchen confidence

Viewers who can follow a recipe but want to understand why steps work so they can adapt without panic.

**Needs**

- technique explained through everyday meals
- permission to use shortcuts when they do not ruin the result
- mistakes shown and corrected calmly

**Constraints**

- embarrassment about basic questions
- fear of wasting ingredients
- sensory uncertainty: “is this browned, reduced, cooked, or seasoned enough?”

**Current alternatives or behaviors**

- relying on exact recipes even when ingredients differ
- asking family, friends, or AI tools for substitutions
- avoiding dishes that seem too technical

## Secondary audiences

- experienced cooks looking for low-stress meal ideas
- parents or caregivers cooking for households with competing preferences
- budget-conscious viewers, if cost guidance remains practical rather than extreme challenge content

## Jobs to be done / desired outcomes

- When deciding what to cook tonight, viewers want a meal that fits their time, ingredients, and energy.
- When following a recipe, they want visual and sensory cues that prevent failure.
- When missing an ingredient, they want to know what can change safely and what cannot.
- When watching food content, they want to feel calmer and more capable afterward.

## Pains, anxieties, and constraints

- recipes that claim “15 minutes” but take 45 with cleanup
- shame around using frozen vegetables, jarred sauces, or pre-cut ingredients
- overproduced food content that entertains but does not help dinner happen
- fear of wasting food or disappointing a household
- unclear measurements, hidden prep, and expensive specialty ingredients

## Motivations

- feed themselves or others with less stress
- save money compared with delivery or prepared meals
- build enough skill to improvise safely
- enjoy cooking as a manageable routine rather than a performance

## Decision criteria

- videos respect the viewer’s time and show real prep/cleanup tradeoffs
- recipes work with supermarket ingredients and common equipment
- host explains substitutions, mistakes, and “good enough” thresholds
- thumbnails and titles are clear without manipulative panic or fake hacks
- comments and community norms remain kind to beginners

## Language and tone

Calm, practical, and encouraging without being sugary. Use “here is the cue,” “if you only have X, do Y,” “this is the cleanup cost,” and “good enough for Tuesday.” Avoid chef superiority, diet moralizing, fake urgency, and “you’ve been doing this wrong your whole life” framing.

## Anti-goals and exclusions

- not for viewers seeking restaurant-level technique as the main value
- not for extreme diet, weight-loss, or body-shaming content
- do not shame convenience ingredients, cultural adaptations, or beginner questions
- do not optimize for viral outrage, dangerous hacks, or misleading recipe times
- do not present unauthentic cultural recipes as definitive without care and sourcing

## Evidence

- Platform observation: many viewers use cooking videos for practical dinner decisions, not only entertainment. Confidence: medium.
- Recipe-content pattern: hidden prep time and unrealistic effort claims are common viewer frustrations. Confidence: high.
- Beginner-learning pattern: visual cues often teach better than measurements alone. Confidence: medium.

## Assumptions

- A calm, usefulness-first channel can build trust even if it grows slower than high-drama formats.
- Showing substitutions and mistakes will improve retention among practical home cooks.
- Viewers will reward honest timing and cleanup notes because they reduce failed attempts.

## Open questions

- What video length best balances calm teaching with weeknight usefulness?
- Should episodes be organized by ingredient, technique, time constraint, or weekly menu?
- How much host personality helps trust before it distracts from the food?
